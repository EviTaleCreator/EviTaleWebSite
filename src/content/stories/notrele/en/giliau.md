## What this story is about

The archangel is born with a mask: her leaves look like a nettle's. At first it's only protection, and she isn't doing anything wrong, she just doesn't tell the truth. But wanting to belong with the strong ones slowly changes her. She starts talking like the nettles, laughing at the weaker ones, hiding the softest part of herself. The turning point comes when, to keep her place among "her own", she betrays her friend, and then lies in their voice. What takes her furthest isn't the lie, though, it's forgetting: she's hidden her flowers for so long that she no longer remembers who she is.

Themes: wanting to belong to a group, pretending, betraying a friend for your own gain, lying, losing yourself.

The endings are different. In the 5–8 version the archangel stays where she grows: the goat only bites off the daisy's flower, the daisy grows a new bud, and the archangel says sorry. In the other versions the girl discovers the archangel and weaves her into a flower crown, and in the 9–12 and Older readers versions the goat eats the daisy as well.

## Ages 2–4

Theme: I don't need to pretend.

For little kids, pretending is usually a game ("I'm a lion!"), and that's fine. The story gently shows the other side: when the archangel keeps pretending to be prickly, everyone runs away from her. But when the girl sees her white flowers, the archangel goes up high with her, into a flower crown, and feels the fresh breeze.

### Questions

- Why did the archangel hiss "ssssss"?
- Where did the archangel go in the end?
- What have you got that's lovely, like the archangel's sweet little flowers?

You can play it together: your child hisses like a nettle, then "opens up their flowers", and you give them a hug. In the game they'll feel the difference without any explaining.

## Ages 5–8

Theme: when you want to be with the "cool kids".

At this age belonging matters a lot: the group of friends in the yard, in class. The archangel isn't bad, she just wants to be with the strong ones, because it's safe there. It's worth talking about what she lost because of it, and that it's never too late to say sorry, even if forgiveness doesn't come straight away.

### Questions

- Why did the archangel want to be like the nettles?
- What did she lose when she hid her flowers?
- How do you think the daisy felt when the archangel pulled her leaves away?

If your child tells you they once laughed along with the others even though they didn't want to, praise them for noticing. You could ask: "What would you have liked to do?"

## Ages 9–12

Theme: when fitting in makes you forget yourself.

Around fifth and sixth grade, the group's rules often start to matter more than friendship. "Let's see whose side you're on" is a real test at this age: are you with us, or with her? The archangel decides in a second, and then lives with it all August. Kids often ask about the ending: is it good or bad that the archangel got picked? Let them think it through themselves, both answers make sense.

### Questions

- When does the archangel first lie? And when does she start lying to herself?
- Why did the nettles say "let's see whose side you're on"? Have you ever heard something like that?
- The girl saw the real archangel, but she picked her. Is the ending happy or sad?

At this age it's hard to admit you've been the one hiding among the nettles yourself. Ask about others instead: "Have you ever seen someone in your class pretend, just to fit in?" They often end up talking about themselves on their own.

## Older readers and adults

The archangel didn't invent her mask. She was born that way, and for a while it's simply convenient: you're not lying, you're just not saying. But the mask works both ways: it protects you from others, and it cuts you off from yourself. The more the archangel fits in, the harder it gets for her to reach the softest part of herself, until in the end she doesn't even remember she has it.

The key moment isn't the betrayal itself, it's the words that come after: "none of my business". What frightens the archangel isn't the lie, it's how naturally it came out in her new voice. That's often how it goes: first you're playing a part, and then you don't notice the part has started speaking for you.

The ending is ambiguous. The archangel is finally seen for who she is, but only once she's been picked. Whether being seen is worth that price, or whether it's her last illusion, the story doesn't say.

### To think about

- In which group or relationship am I not speaking in my own voice?
- What have I been hiding in myself for so long that I no longer remember I have it?
- When was the last time I "pulled my leaves away" to keep my place among my own?
